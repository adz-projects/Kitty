# Critique guide: proposal

A grant application, fellowship statement or project proposal persuades
reviewers, who are reading many at once, to fund one thing. Reviewers judge
against stated criteria and often decide on the first page. If the brief
names the funder or the call, check the draft against that call's own review
criteria; if it names none, ask the author for them and use the general
criteria below meanwhile.

## Settings for the core lenses

- **Multivalence: moderate.** The prize is preliminary evidence that is at
  once proof the idea works, proof this team can do it, and the vivid case
  that makes the problem real.
- **Authority:** demonstrated capability: preliminary results, access,
  prior work. A list of credentials is weaker than one result.
- **The turn: none.** The gap and the approach should be clear from the
  first paragraph.
- **Signposts:** reviewers navigate by headings and aim numbers; keep them.
- **Ending:** the payoff — what will be different if this is funded.
- **The adversary:** the sceptical reviewer. Every risk they would raise
  should be named with its alternative.

## Anatomy: the aims page (or its equivalent)

For each part: where it is (P#), present, missing or misplaced.

1. **Hook.** The importance of the problem, concretely: who is affected and
   how much.
2. **Gap.** What is not known or not solved, and why it matters that it is
   not.
3. **Long-term goal.** The larger programme this work serves.
4. **Objective.** What this proposal will accomplish, in one sentence.
5. **Central hypothesis or rationale.** Why this approach should work, and
   the preliminary evidence for it.
6. **Aims (usually two or three).** Each with its approach and expected
   outcome.
   - Are the aims related but independent, so the second survives if the
     first fails?
   - Is each aim specific enough to know when it is done?
7. **Expected outcomes and payoff.** What will exist or be known at the end,
   and its impact.

## Lens: the review panel's read

1. **Criteria.** For each review criterion — by default significance,
   innovation, approach, the team, and the environment — where does the
   draft make its case (P#)? Which criterion is weakest?
2. **Feasibility against ambition.** Is the scope achievable in the time and
   budget proposed? Name what a reviewer would call overreach.
3. **Risks and alternatives.** For each major risk, is there a named fallback?
4. **The one-page test.** Could a reviewer who reads only the first page say
   what will be done, why it matters, and why this team? What would they get
   wrong?
5. **Mixed panels.** Jargon that a reviewer outside the subfield would not
   know.
