# Critique guide: op-ed

An op-ed is an argument for a general, impatient reader, usually 600–1,200
words, sold to an editor on three questions: why this, why now, why you. It
has a recognisable multipart structure. Critique the structure first: a
well-written op-ed with a missing part does not get published.

## Settings for the core lenses

- **Multivalence: high.** The word limit means most body paragraphs should do
  two jobs or more. The lede in particular should be TEXTURE + STAKES, and
  ideally show AUTHORITY too. A body paragraph that is only EVIDENCE is a
  candidate for compression.
- **Evidence:** compressed and emblematic. One sharply rendered case carries
  more than five citations. Sources are attributed inline ("a 2024 Pew survey
  found"), never footnoted.
- **Authority:** staked presence. The writer speaks as someone inside the
  problem, not as an institutional "we". AUTHORITY:stated in a bio-like
  sentence is a flag when a shown detail could do the job.
- **The turn:** if the piece reframes its premise, it must land by about the
  60% mark without feeling like a bait-and-switch.
- **Signposts:** cut aggressively; the form rewards clean cuts over bridges.
- **Ending:** returns to the lede's image or peg, or makes a specific ask of a
  reachable actor, or widens to the harder question. Not a generic "we must".

## Anatomy

For each part: where it is (P#), whether it is present, missing or misplaced,
and the answers to its questions.

1. **Lede / hook (P1–P2).** A concrete moment, fact or scene tied to the news
   peg.
   - Is the peg named, and is it timely? Would the piece still run a week from
     now — is there a durable reason as well as a topical one?
   - Is the opening the most arresting concrete thing in the piece? If a later
     paragraph holds a better one, name it.
   - Does it take more than two paragraphs to reach the argument?
2. **Thesis (by P3, at the latest P4).** One sentence, arguable and specific.
   - Quote it. Could an editor write the headline from it?
   - Is it buried in a hedge, split across sentences, or stated only as a
     question?
3. **Stakes, and why you.** Why this reader should care, and why this writer
   is the one to say it.
   - Are the stakes concrete (who loses what) or abstract ("our democracy")?
   - Is the writer's standing shown through what they know, or stated?
4. **Argument (the body; usually two or three moves).** Each move: one claim,
   one emblematic piece of evidence.
   - Are the moves distinct, or the same point three times?
   - Is each move's evidence the most vivid available, or the most
     respectable?
   - Is there a catalogue where one example should carry the weight?
5. **"To be sure" (the counter).** Names the strongest objection, concedes
   what is true in it, answers it briefly.
   - Is it the strongest objection for this readership, or a token?
   - Is it placed before the final third, so the ending is not spent
     rebutting?
   - Does the concession cost the writer anything? A concession that concedes
     nothing reads as a strawman.
6. **Close / kicker.** The last paragraph and especially its last line.
   - Does it return to the lede, make a specific ask, or widen the frame? Or
     does it restate the thesis?
   - Is any ask addressed to someone who can act on it?
   - Would the last line work read aloud as the end?

## Lens: the editor's read

Answer as an op-ed editor deciding whether to run it.

1. **The pitch.** Write the two-sentence pitch this draft implies. If you
   cannot, say what is missing.
2. **So what / who cares / why now / why you.** One line each, with the P#
   that answers it, or "unanswered".
3. **Word budget.** Count the words. Flag more than 10% over 1,200, or the
   target the brief gives. Estimate the share spent before the thesis and on
   background; flag more than a fifth on either.
4. **Form.** Paragraphs over four sentences; academic throat-clearing ("This
   essay argues"); footnote-style sourcing; jargon the general reader will
   not know.
5. **The other side's editor.** Would an editor at an outlet that disagrees
   find the "to be sure" honest? Why or why not?
