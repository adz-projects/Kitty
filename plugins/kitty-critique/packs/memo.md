# Critique guide: memo

A memo (also a work email, briefing, update, or decision request) is written
for a busy reader who will skim it and must act on it. Critique it with the
six principles of Todd Rogers and Jessica Lasky-Fink's *Writing for Busy
Readers*. Elegance is beside the point: the test is whether the reader
understands, and responds, with the least effort.

## Settings for the core lenses

- **Multivalence: low.** One idea per paragraph. Do not ask a paragraph to do
  several jobs; the reader should never have to decode what it is for. The one
  welcome exception is a concrete number or example that carries the stakes
  and the writer's credibility at once.
- **The turn: none.** A memo should not surprise its reader. A reframing late
  in the memo is a defect: move it to the top.
- **Signposts:** headings and labels that help the reader navigate are good
  here. Do not cut them; cut only transitions that add words without aiding
  navigation.
- **Altitude:** concrete and specific throughout. Abstraction is a cost.
- **Ending:** the ask, with what the reader needs to act on it. Not an
  aperture.
- **The adversary:** the reader's likely objection or competing priority,
  answered briefly where it arises.

## Anatomy

For each part: where it is (P#), present, missing or misplaced.

1. **Subject line, or title.** Specific: it states the decision, the finding,
   or the ask, and any deadline. "Budget update" is a flag; "Approve $40K for
   Q3 hiring by Friday" is the standard.
2. **Bottom line up front (first one to three sentences).** The
   recommendation, the decision needed, and by when.
3. **Context.** Only what the reader needs in order to decide.
4. **Options or analysis.** Each option with its consequence; the comparison
   in a table where there are several dimensions.
5. **Recommendation.**
6. **The ask and next steps.** Who does what, by when, and how.

## Lens: the six principles

Test the draft against each principle. Quote the passages that break it.

1. **Less is more.**
   - *Fewer words:* count them. What could go without loss to the reader?
   - *Fewer ideas:* which ideas does the reader not need in order to act?
   - *Fewer requests:* more than one ask dilutes each. If there are several,
     which matters most?
2. **Make reading easy.**
   - Short, common words; flag jargon and unexplained acronyms.
   - Straightforward sentences; flag sentences over 25 words or with more
     than one subordinate clause.
   - Active voice that names who acts.
3. **Design for easy navigation.**
   - Is the key information immediately visible, in the first lines?
   - Are distinct ideas in separate paragraphs, and related ideas together?
   - Are ideas ordered by priority to the reader?
   - Do headings say something ("Why the budget fell short"), or only label
     ("Background")?
   - Would a list, table or visual make a comparison easier?
4. **Use enough formatting, but no more.**
   - Does the formatting match what this reader expects in this medium?
   - Are the most important ideas highlighted, bolded or underlined, and
     only those? If much is bold, nothing is.
5. **Tell readers why they should care.**
   - Does the memo say what matters to this reader — its consequences for
     them — rather than what matters to the writer?
   - If it goes to several readers, does it say which of them must act?
6. **Make responding easy.**
   - Are the steps to act simple and few?
   - Is everything needed to act (deadline, link, contact, form) gathered in
     one place?
   - Could a reader respond in one step?

## Lens: the busy reader's read

1. Read only the subject line and the first two sentences. What does the
   reader now know, and could they act? Say what is missing.
2. If the reader reads only the bold text and the headings, what do they
   take away?
