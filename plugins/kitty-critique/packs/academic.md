# Critique guide: academic article

An academic article makes a contribution to a conversation among peers, and
is judged first on whether that contribution is clear, real and defended.
Critique the contribution before the prose. The piece may be empirical
(introduction, methods, results, discussion) or interpretive (an argument
through a body of evidence); the anatomy below covers both, and the brief
should say which.

## Settings for the core lenses

- **Multivalence: moderate.** The prize is evidence that is also the
  contribution: the case, passage or result that both proves the claim and is
  the new thing the paper adds. A well-chosen case is also the paper's
  texture.
- **Evidence:** methodical; the craft is in how it is narrated. The most
  contested evidence deserves the most space.
- **Authority:** intellectual honesty. Limits named where the data actually
  resisted, not a boilerplate limitations paragraph.
- **The turn:** unfolds across sections. The literature review can set up the
  frame that the findings overturn.
- **Signposts:** tolerated more than elsewhere — reviewers navigate by them —
  but meta-discourse ("In this section, I will…") more than once per section
  is a flag. Negative space only once the logic is airtight, so reviewers do
  not mistake a cut for an omission.
- **Ending:** the contribution restated in new terms, its implications, and
  the open question it creates. No new evidence.

## Anatomy

For each part: where it is (P# or section), present, missing or misplaced,
and the answers to its questions.

1. **Title and abstract.**
   - Does the abstract give the problem, the gap, the approach, the finding
     and the contribution?
   - Does it promise what the paper actually delivers?
2. **Introduction: Swales's CARS moves.**
   - *Move 1, establish the territory:* why the topic matters; what is known.
     Is it brisk, or a survey?
   - *Move 2, establish a niche:* the gap, counter-claim, or open question.
     Is it a real gap, or only "no one has studied X in setting Y"? Why does
     the gap matter?
   - *Move 3, occupy the niche:* the purpose, the questions or hypothesis, the
     principal finding, and the paper's value. Is the contribution stated in
     one sentence by the end of the introduction? Is the finding given early,
     as most fields expect, or withheld?
3. **Literature and positioning.**
   - Is the literature synthesised by idea, or catalogued by author ("Smith
     argues… Jones argues…")?
   - Does the paper argue with the literature, taking a position in it, or
     only review it?
   - Does it set up the frame the paper will complicate?
4. **Methods, materials, or approach.**
   - Is there enough to evaluate and reproduce the work? Are the choices
     justified, not just described?
   - For interpretive work: why this archive, corpus or set of cases?
5. **Results, or the analysis.**
   - Does each section state its claim up front?
   - Is evidence narrated, or dumped in tables and block quotes without
     interpretation?
6. **Discussion.**
   - Does it read the findings back against the literature from part 3?
   - Are alternative explanations considered and answered?
   - Are limits specific to where the evidence resisted?
7. **Conclusion.** See the ending setting above.

## Lens: the reviewer's read

Answer as a demanding but fair peer reviewer.

1. **Contribution consistency.** Quote the contribution as stated in the
   abstract, the introduction and the conclusion. Do the three agree? Does
   the evidence support the strongest of them?
2. **The reviewer's first objection.** What would reviewer 2 say first? Is it
   pre-empted?
3. **Terms.** Are key terms defined once and used consistently? Quote any
   term used in two senses.
4. **Hedging calibration.** Where is a claim hedged more than its evidence
   warrants, and where less? Quote both kinds.
5. **Proportion.** Estimate the share of words in each part. Flag a literature
   review longer than the analysis, or an analysis that gives equal space to
   strong and weak evidence.
