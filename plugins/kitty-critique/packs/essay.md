# Critique guide: essay

The essayistic piece — a long-form essay, a reflective academic essay, an
essayistic op-ed — honours the contracts of argument (rigour, evidence, clear
stakes, respect for the reader's time) while letting the reader watch a mind
test an idea against reality. A verdict piece announces, defends and
restates; an essay inquires, complicates and turns. Critique for inquiry.

## Settings for the core lenses

- **Multivalence: high.** The best essays render evidence as scene, and let
  the scene establish the writer's authority. Single-job paragraphs are the
  essay's commonest weakness.
- **Authority:** situated. Experience and expertise are a lens, not a
  credential: where the writer stands, where they are implicated, where their
  own thinking met resistance.
- **The turn:** essential, past the midpoint ("we thought this was a problem
  of X; it is a problem of Y").
- **Signposts:** cut. Juxtaposition and negative space do the work.
- **Ending:** opens the aperture. End a beat early, or widen onto the harder
  question the argument has now put on the table. A tidy normative bow is a
  flag.

## Anatomy

The essay's structure follows the movement of thought rather than a template,
but it has parts. For each: where it is (P#), and the answers to its
questions.

1. **Opening.** A question, a scene, or a premise.
   - Does the opening make a promise of inquiry, or announce a verdict?
   - Is it concrete (ladder level 1–2)?
2. **Working premise.** What the essay sets out believing.
   - Is it stated plainly enough that its later complication is felt?
3. **Complication.** Evidence that resists the premise.
   - Is the resistance real, or staged so it can be knocked down?
   - Is the most contested piece of evidence given the most room?
4. **The turn (volta).** The reframing, past the midpoint.
   - Quote the sentence where it happens. Is it earned by what came before?
   - Is it telegraphed ("But something more interesting was happening")?
5. **Dwelling.** After the turn, the essay slows on what the reframing
   reveals.
   - Does it dwell, or hurry to a conclusion?
6. **Ending.** See the settings above. Run the penultimate-beat test with
   special care.

## Lens: the essayist's craft

1. **A worthy adversary.** Does the essay argue with the strongest,
   most sympathetic version of the view it resists, from inside the writer's
   own community, and show the exact seam where it gives way?
2. **Altitude control.** Does the essay move deliberately between ground level
   and theory? Name the longest stretch at high altitude and the best
   ground-level anchor to expand.
3. **Self-implication.** Where does the writer acknowledge where they stand
   and what they are implicated in? Where does an institutional "we" or a
   credential stand in for that? Ask, never supply.
4. **Juxtaposition and negative space.** Where are two contrasting facts or
   scenes separated by explanation that could go, so the reader feels the
   spark in the gap?
5. **Pacing and compression.** What routine background could become a brisk
   sentence, to buy room for the sharpest evidence?
6. **The motif.** Is there a recurring concrete image, metaphor or question?
   List each return (P#) and say whether it accumulates meaning or only
   repeats. If there is none, is there a candidate already in the draft?
