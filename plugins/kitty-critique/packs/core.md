# Critique guide: core lenses

These lenses apply to any argumentative or explanatory prose. The genre pack
adjusts them (how much multivalence the genre wants, where the turn belongs,
what the ending owes the reader) and adds the genre's own anatomy and lenses.

## Rules for every lens

- Diagnose; never rewrite the draft. A suggested move is a sentence of
  direction, not replacement prose.
- Ground every finding in a paragraph number from the map and a verbatim
  quote: `- [P7] "exact words from the draft" — what is wrong or strong, and
  why → the move, or a question for the author.`
- At most 8 findings per lens, the most consequential first. No generic praise
  and no writing-centre advice ("consider varying your sentences").
- Never invent facts, sources or experiences for the author. Where a fix needs
  something only the author knows, say so and ask the question that would
  surface it.
- Judge against the brief: its reader, its purpose, its stage, and its
  preservation lock. Nothing a lens suggests may flatten what the lock
  protects.

## Lens: map

The map is done first; every other lens uses its numbering.

1. Number the paragraphs P1, P2, … in order. A heading is not a paragraph:
   list it as `H: <heading text>` where it falls. A block quote or list
   belongs to the paragraph that introduces it.
2. For each paragraph give one line:
   `P# | "first six words…" | part: <genre anatomy part> | jobs: <JOBS> | does: <what it does, in under 15 words>`
3. Tag its jobs from this vocabulary (most paragraphs have one to three):
   - **CLAIM** — advances the piece's position.
   - **EVIDENCE** — supports a claim: data, a source, a case, a quotation.
   - **TEXTURE** — concrete, on-the-ground material interesting for its own
     sake: a scene, an object, a voice, a surprising particular.
   - **AUTHORITY** — shows why this writer can be trusted here. Mark it
     **AUTHORITY:shown** when it comes through what the writer notices or how
     the evidence is handled, **AUTHORITY:stated** when it is a credential,
     title or years of service.
   - **STAKES** — why it matters, to whom, and why now.
   - **ADVERSARY** — engages an objection, an opposing view, or a limit.
   - **MOTIF** — returns to a recurring image, phrase or question.
   - **FRAME** — orients: background, definition, roadmap, transition.
   - **ASK** — tells the reader to do or decide something.
4. After the table, in one line each: where the central claim is first stated
   (P#); where the piece turns or reframes its premise (P#, or "none"); the
   paragraphs doing three or more jobs; the paragraphs doing only one.

## Lens: multivalence

Strong published prose makes one move do several jobs: a piece of evidence
that is also an arresting particular and also the proof that the writer knows
the ground. How much the genre wants this is set in its pack. Using the map:

1. **Single-job evidence.** Which evidence only proves? For each, could it
   also give texture or show authority if it were rendered more concretely,
   from material already in the draft? If the material would have to come from
   the author, say what to ask them for.
2. **Stated authority.** Where is authority stated rather than shown? Is there
   a detail elsewhere in the draft that could carry it instead, so the
   credential can go?
3. **Decorative texture.** Which concrete material carries no part of the
   argument? Connect it to a claim, or cut it.
4. **The best opportunity.** Name the one example that could do three jobs if
   it were told differently, and say which three and how.
5. **Overload.** Is any paragraph doing so many jobs that a reader cannot tell
   what it is for?

## Lens: argument and adversary

1. Quote the sentence closest to the central claim. Is it arguable: could an
   informed member of the reader's community disagree? Is it specific enough
   to be wrong?
2. For each major claim, does its evidence support that claim, or something
   nearby and weaker? Quote both sides of every leap.
3. What must the reader already grant for the argument to work? Name the
   unstated assumptions that carry weight.
4. Who or what does the draft argue against now? For this reader, is that an
   easy villain or a token objection?
5. Build the hardest, most sympathetic objection, from someone inside the
   author's own community who still disagrees, in two or three sentences.
   Does the draft meet it? Where exactly does the draft's case give way to it,
   if it does?
6. Where is the author implicated in what the piece criticises, or where did
   their own thinking meet resistance? Quote the places where an
   institutional "we" or a credential stands in for that friction, and ask the
   author one sharp question about each. Never supply the answer.

## Lens: reader and altitude

1. For the reader named in the brief: where would they first get lost, and
   where first get bored? Quote both. List undefined terms and assumed
   knowledge.
2. **Topic-sentence test.** Read only the first sentence of each paragraph, in
   order. Does that sequence make the argument? Where does it break?
3. **Altitude.** Give each paragraph a level on this ladder:
   1 a concrete scene, sensory detail, object, or verbatim primary source;
   2 a specific anecdote, a named person's practice, a concrete mechanism;
   3 a specific case, finding, or localised data point;
   4 an institutional trend, policy, or sector-wide claim;
   5 an ideology, theory, or normative generalisation.
   Write the ladder as `P1(2) P2(4) …`.
4. Flag **stalls**: three or more consecutive paragraphs at 4–5.
5. Flag **catalogues**: three or more consecutive paragraphs presenting
   parallel examples at roughly equal weight. Name the one most worth
   dwelling on and the ones to compress into a sentence.
6. **Pacing.** Where does the draft spend words on routine background, and
   where does it hurry past its most contested point?

## Lens: seams and ending

1. **Signposts.** Up to five explicit transitions or throat-clearing phrases
   ("Finally,", "This shows that", "It is important to note") whose deletion
   would put two things in sharper juxtaposition. For each, quote it and say
   what the cut unlocks. Leave alone any signpost the genre pack says the
   reader needs for navigation.
2. **The turn.** Locate the paragraph where the draft reframes its own
   premise. Is it telegraphed, does it land cleanly, or is there none? Judge
   it against where the genre pack says the turn belongs, if it wants one.
3. **Ending.** Quote the last sentence of the final paragraph and of the one
   before it. Mentally delete the final paragraph: does the piece end better?
   Verdict: KEEP, CUT or COMPRESS the final paragraph, against what the genre
   pack says the ending owes the reader.

## Lens: prose

Run only at the polish stage, or when the brief asks for it.

1. Stock and machine-sounding phrasing: "in an era of", "it's not just X, it's
   Y", "underscores", "delve", "landscape", "tapestry", "navigate the
   complexities", tidy moralising closers. Quote each.
2. Sentences over 35 words that tangle; stacked hedges; nominalisations that
   hide a verb ("make a decision"); passives that hide who acted.
3. Rhythm: runs of three or more sentences of the same length and shape.
4. Lines that are the writer at their best. Quote two or three: the revision
   must protect them.

## Generic anatomy (genre "other")

For prose that fits none of the packs (a report, a blog post, a newsletter,
a talk):

1. **Opening promise** — what the first paragraph tells the reader they will
   get. Is it specific? Is it the most interesting true thing about the piece?
2. **Delivery** — does the body deliver exactly that promise, in an order the
   reader can follow? What does it deliver that was never promised?
3. **Ending** — does it complete the promise, or restate it?

Multivalence: moderate. Turn: optional. Ending: completes the promise.
